## Context

What we were trying to do, and what state the system was in. Two or three
sentences. Enough that a reader six months from now knows where this sits.

## The problem

What actually blocked us. Be specific — an error message, a number, a constraint
someone stated. "It was slow" is not a problem statement; "the backfill of one
day took 40 minutes, so 4,000 days would take 111 days" is.

## Why it was hard

What made this a judgement call rather than a lookup. Usually one of: two
requirements in genuine conflict, a constraint discovered late, an option that
looked right but had a hidden cost, or missing information that could not be
obtained in time.

## Decision

What we chose and — the part that matters — **what we gave up by choosing it**.
An architect who cannot name the downside of their own decision has not made one.

## How we validated it

The test, measurement or observation that told us the decision was working.
If there wasn't one, say so; that is itself worth knowing.

## Outcome

What actually happened. Numbers where they exist. If this is not yet observed,
write `Not yet observed — decision taken <date>, will be validated in <phase>.`
Do not write an outcome that has not happened.

## What I'd do differently

The honest retrospective. Interviewers weight this heavily — it separates
people who learned from people who merely survived.

---

## 90-second answer

> The spoken version. Around 200-250 words. Constraint, stakes, options, the
> call and its cost, and how you knew it worked. No jargon that a hiring manager
> outside your specialism would not follow.
